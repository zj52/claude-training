Part 1 · Purpose & the Cornell AI ecosystem

Why we’re here.

Another AI tool, what it’s for, the rules for what can go in it, and where it fits among Cornell’s AI tools.

The goal

Our goal is to help create efficiency by automating tedious work with AI.

Not to replace your judgment.

Think of Claude as an assistant for the boring, repeatable parts of the job: pulling information out of different places, putting a rough draft together, looking something up.

Our goal today is to get oriented to using it, understand where it sits in the Cornell AI ecosystem, and try out some examples together.

Live tool status: AI Innovation Hub.

The tool

Claude Desktop*

Set up to use models in our secure AI infrastructure:

  • Uses the Cowork agent to do tasks on files in an isolated virtual machine (VM) on your computer.
  • Reads and writes one configured folder; others need your explicit permission each time.
  • Pay-per-use; most subscription features included.

* Anthropic calls this configuration “Claude Desktop with third-party (3P) inference,” or Claude 3P for short. You’ll see that name in these materials; it’s the same tool.

Data rules

Same rules as Sandbox AI.

  • OK to use. Confidential Cornell data used in research administration, such as protocols, awards, proposals, and IP.
  • Not allowed. DoD work, CUI (controlled unclassified information), export-controlled or government-restricted information, and HIPAA data (we shouldn’t encounter it).
  • Claude 3P is a different front end pointing at the same infrastructure as Sandbox AI, the LLM API Gateway to the cloud models Cornell already has data-protection contracts with.
  • Web search is new, and safe. It runs through Gemini’s enterprise grounding, so your prompt isn’t logged.

If you can put it in Sandbox AI, you can put it in Claude 3P. Same gateway, same contracted infrastructure.

Please feed Claude

  • Any of the documents you use in your day-to-day work
  • Awards
  • Proposals
  • Intellectual property
  • Other confidential material

Please do not feed

  • DoD work
  • Export-controlled data
  • HIPAA data

How AI works.

Three ideas: answers are probabilistic, working memory is limited, and an agent does the task rather than describing it.

How AI works

Probabilistic, not deterministic.

An LLM builds its answer one token at a time, picking among plausible next words by probability. There is no fixed right answer — only what’s most likely given what came before.

That’s why two runs of the same prompt can come back worded differently, and occasionally with different substance. It’s a feature, not a bug — but it’s why you read every answer critically and spot-check the things that matter.

It also answers even when it doesn’t know. Where the model has a knowledge gap, prediction fills it with plausible-sounding text. That concentrates in the specifics — names, numbers, dates, citations — so those get checked first.

How AI works

Working memory is limited.

Everything Claude is holding for you — your instructions, the files, the conversation so far — lives in one finite working memory called the context window. When it fills up, quality slips in predictable ways.

Limited space

It fills up

Long documents and long chats compete for the same room. A fresh chat for a fresh task isn’t a workaround; it’s the right habit.

Buried details

The middle fades

Key information from the middle of a long session gets less attention than the start and the end. Repeat what matters when you change direction.

Drift

Instructions wear off

Rules you set early lose their grip as a session runs long, which can make a conversation hard to steer. Restate what matters, or start a new session and restate the task.

How AI works

A chatbot answers. An agent does the task.

First-generation AI chat took one prompt and returned one answer. An agent works in a loop: it plans, uses tools, looks at what came back, and keeps going until the job is done.

1 · Plans

Breaks the job down

Turns your request into steps and decides what to do first.

2 · Uses tools

Acts, then checks

Reads and writes files, searches, calls connectors like eCFR, runs skills — and reviews its own results as it goes.

3 · Delivers

Finished files, not a reply

The output is the work product itself — documents, redlines, spreadsheets — saved where you can use them.

Claude 3P is agentic. The contract review in today’s workshop is one run of an agent, start to finish.

Your installation

What comes with the RAIS setup.

Your installation is configured for 3P use and comes with a few items that are specific to our work. We’ll go over each one.

Two ways to work

Live text of the Code of Federal Regulations, pulled from the official source at the moment you ask. Always current, no stale copies.

Plugin

Legal Plugin

Contract skills built around the OSP playbook and Cornell’s standard research contract. One review produces the memo, the redline, the letter, the approval memo, and the tracker.

We’ll use the eCFR connector and the Legal Plugin in the workshop.

The interface

What you see when you open Claude 3P.

  • Chat box. Where you type your prompt and drop in files.
  • Projects. Your unit’s instructions and reference files, bundled. Start chats inside the project and the Legal Plugin skills and eCFR connector are already on hand.
  • Model picker (bottom). Sonnet is the right choice for everyday work. Switch to Opus for long documents and hard reasoning; each step up costs more, and your unit is billed for usage.
  • File drop. Drag and drop — PDFs, images, Word, Excel, CSV, code files all work.
  • Recent chats (sidebar). Your previous chats, stored on your local machine until you delete them.

Appendix · OSP Legal Plugin · review memo, page 2 of 15

How to read the findings table.

1 2 3 4 5
  1. 1
    The section column. Every problem is labelled with the company’s own section number and listed in their order, so the memo can be read next to their contract.
  2. 2
    Grade. RED means it conflicts with Cornell’s rules. YELLOW means there is room to negotiate. “Absence” means a protection Cornell’s own standard contract has and theirs leaves out.
  3. 3
    Tier. Tier 1: we cannot sign it as written. Tier 2: push hard. Tier 3: live with it if we have to. The tier, not the grade, tells you whether signing is blocked.
  4. 4
    Who decides. The office that has to weigh in. Anything about patents or inventions goes to the Center for Technology Licensing. A dash means the officer on the award can handle it alone.
  5. 5
    The references in brackets. Notes like “(SRA 17)” point to the matching part of Cornell’s own standard research contract, so you can see what the problem was measured against.

Appendix · OSP Legal Plugin · marked-up contract, page 8 of 24

How to read the tracked changes.

1 2 3 4 5
  1. 1
    It is their document. Their letterhead, their numbering, their footer. Cornell edited the company’s own file instead of sending back a Cornell form, which makes it easy for them to reply.
  2. 2
    Crossed-out text is what comes out. Their section 9(C) said the university “hereby grants” the license. That would make this contract itself the license, which our office has no authority to sign.
  3. 3
    Underlined text is what goes in. The replacement is a promise to grant a license later, on terms worked out with Cornell’s licensing office. The company still gets its license; it just comes from the office that is allowed to give it.
  4. 4
    The line in the margin. Word’s marker for an edit. All 57 edits are signed “Cornell OSP” and dated, so their lawyers can accept or reject them one at a time.
  5. 5
    A limit, not a refusal. Section 9(D) keeps the company’s exclusive rights but puts boundaries on them: an agreed area of use, no passing it on to anyone else, costs and fee set by Cornell.

How the asks are worded.

Appendix · OSP Legal Plugin · reviewing the output

Three things worth checking.

1Grounding

Cornell’s rules, or generic contract habits?

Every grade should trace back to Cornell policy, our standard research contract, or the OSP playbook. Not ordinary vendor-contract norms that do not apply to a university.

2Escalation

Does it go somewhere that exists?

It should name offices Cornell actually has: the licensing office, risk management, sponsored financial services, the export control officer, university relations, university counsel, the OSP director. Not a chief executive, not an outside law firm.

3Consistency

Does the markup say what the memo says?

The 57 edits should match the memo’s findings, and the spreadsheet should admit where a problem is only partly fixed instead of showing it closed.

Prepared by Cornell’s Office of Sponsored Programs as a review aid. Not legal advice. If all three hold, this is usable as a starting point for the officer on the award — a first pass, not a substitute for judgment.

  7. 7
